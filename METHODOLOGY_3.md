# Methodology

I forecast the estimated avoidable deaths caused by emergency-department admission delays in the Bristol NHS system, ten days ahead, across all 173 windows of the assessment period. The approach follows from the behaviour of the target: day to day it is less a trending series than a slowly moving level with a weekly rhythm and substantial noise on top. I therefore do not predict the deaths directly, but decompose the task into a component that can be estimated stably and a smaller correction. The two prize horizons, days 1 to 5 and days 6 to 10, are modelled separately.

Data preparation is carried out in R and produces a single daily table. The raw variables are recorded at frequencies ranging from quarter-hourly to daily, so I aggregate them onto a shared daily grid, assigning each reading to a "midday day": values recorded up to noon are counted toward the current day and later values toward the next. This is done to respect the rule that a forecast on day D may use data only up to midday on D. I then add calendar terms and rolling summaries of the operational predictors. The target is handled separately because it is reported with a three-day lag: any feature derived from it is built from a series shifted back three days, so that no value is used before it would have been available.

The forecast is constructed in three layers. The first is a baseline: a day-of-week-adjusted rolling mean of the lagged target, which is simple and fully causal. The second is a correction. I use TabPFN, a pretrained transformer for tabular data that requires no conventional training and runs quickly on a CPU, to predict the residual rather than the deaths themselves, fitting one model per horizon on the preceding year and supplying the operational signals associated with short-term pressure: patients in the department, decisions to admit, ambulance handovers, escalation beds, and waiting-list backlogs. The two are combined as baseline plus alpha times the residual. Because the residual is largely noise, an over-confident correction tends to increase error, so alpha is tuned per block and may fall to zero. In the submitted configuration days 1 to 5 use alpha 0.8, while days 6 to 10 retain the baseline alone, as the operational signal is by then too stale to contribute.

These choices were settled using rolling-origin validation, weighted toward the October-to-March months so that the validation season matches the assessment season. This is what revealed the difference between the horizons, with the residual improving the near term but not the far. For the assessment run the pipeline walks forward through every legal origin, refitting on the most recent year at each step. The code remains fixed and only the data passing through it changes, which keeps the entry within the recalibration rule and well under the one-hour limit.
